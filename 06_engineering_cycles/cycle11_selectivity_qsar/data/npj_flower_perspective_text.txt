Accelerating GPCR drug discovery
through computation and experiment
integrated with direct detection of ligand
binding events
Check for updates
Judith Su1 , Stephen B. Liggett2, Soo-Kyung Kim3, Donghwa Kim2 & William A. Goddard III3
We present a perspective on an integrated workflow for GPCR drug discovery that combines
computational modeling, functional cellular assays, and FLOWER, a label-free ultra-sensitive optical
biosensing system for quantitating direct ligand receptor binding. We use bitter and sweet taste
receptors (TAS2Rs and T1R2/T1R3) as examples of how FLOWER resolves receptor activation
mechanisms left ambiguous after in silico screening and cell assays. This workflow offers a
generalizable strategy for accelerating the discovery of selective and mechanism informed GPCR
targeting therapeutics.
G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the largest and most functionally
diverse family of membrane proteins in the human genome, targeted by
over 30% of FDA-approved drugs1
. Despite their importance, drug discovery for many GPCRs, particularly those outside of class A, remains
challenging due to conformational complexity, limited structural
information2
, and the difficulty of experimentally measuring ligand binding
kinetics and mechanisms under near-native conditions3
.
Over the past several years, major methodological advances have
begun to reshape early-stage GPCR drug discovery. These include improved
structure prediction tools such asAlphaFold24 and GPCR-specificmodeling
frameworks, the rapid expansion of high-resolution cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures5 for active and inactive GPCR states6
, and
enhanced computational docking7 and molecular dynamics approaches8 for
predicting binding energetics and conformational transitions. On the
experimental side, cell-based assays incorporating mini-G proteins9
, conformational biosensors10, and engineered signaling reporters11 provide
mechanistically informative functional readouts relevant to efficacy, bias,
and selectivity. Finally, sensitive biophysical methods including biolayer
interferometry (BLI)12, and emerging optical biosensing platforms13 now
allow direct measurement of ligand-protein binding interactions with
increasing precision. In parallel, these developments enable earlier and more
informed decision-making in GPCR ligand optimization.
In this Perspective, we describe an integrated workflow that combines in
silico computational modeling of huge data bases ( > 100 million), functional
cellular assays on thousands of ligands, followed by direct ligand-protein
kinetic binding measurements via the FLOWER (Frequency Locked Optical
Whispering Evanescent Resonator) technique14–16, which together accelerate
and refine discovery efforts (Fig. 1). Unlike conventional workflows in which
computational predictions are validated only indirectly through cell-based
outcomes, the strategy described here directly links in silico modeling with in
vitro measurements obtained from FLOWER. In our current implementation, FLOWER provides direct, label-free quantification of receptor–ligand
binding affinity under near-physiological conditions, enabling computational
predictions of binding strength and selectivity to be tested against experimentally determined equilibrium constants (Kd) without the use of tags.
We highlight the workflow using case studies of two structurally and
functionally distinct GPCR families: the bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs), a
class of orphan-like receptors with atypical activation features, and the sweet
taste receptor (T1R2/T1R3), a class C GPCR heterodimer with multiple
allosteric and orthosteric binding sites. These systems illustrate how the
workflow can resolve mechanistic ambiguities, characterize ligand bias, and
guide rational lead optimization, building on methodologies we have previously established in separate studies. While focused on chemosensory
GPCRs, the approach is generalizable across the broader GPCR landscape.
The goal of this Perspective is to outline how these complementary methodologies can be integrated into a cohesive, iterative workflow to accelerate
early-stage GPCR ligand discovery.

Mechanism of detecting ligand-receptor interactions
using FLOWER
Direct and quantitative characterization of protein–ligand interactions is
essential for verifying computational predictions, identifying unusual
binding actions, and confirming promising lead compounds targeting
GPCRs from cell-based systems. One method that enables such measurements with high sensitivity and specificity is FLOWER13–16,21–31. FLOWER is
a label-free optical biosensing platform that tracks refractive index changes
induced by molecular binding on the surface of ultra-high-Q whispering
gallery mode (WGM) optical resonators (Fig. 2a).
WGM resonators confine light by circulating it along a curved
dielectric surface32–34, where it repeatedly interacts with the surrounding
medium through a tightly confined evanescent field. This architecture
allows FLOWER to detect minute refractive index changes in real time35

Although FLOWER can be implemented using various cavity geometries,
including microspheres, microdisks, and integrated photonic ring resonators, we typically employ ultra-high-Q silica microtoroid resonators due to
their exceptional quality factors ( > 108
), small mode volumes that enhance
light–matter interactions, strong and accessible evanescent fields for
surface-sensitive detection, and compact on-chip format. These attributes
enhance detection sensitivity and allow robust, reproducible measurements
of ligand binding events. In a typical experiment, laser light is evanescently
coupled into the microtoroid36,37, where it circulates hundreds of thousands
of times—akin to a whisper echoing within a circular gallery. The system
actively locks the laser frequency to the resonant frequency of the microtoroid using a feedback control loop. When a molecule binds to the resonator surface, the local refractive index changes slightly, shifting the
resonant frequency. The feedback system continuously tracks this shift in
real time, allowing precise and dynamic quantification of molecular interactions. From the magnitude and kinetics of these frequency shifts,
FLOWER can extract key binding parameters. As shown in Fig. 2b, the
concentration-dependent peak resonance shifts can be used to estimate
apparent binding constants by plotting the maximal response versus ligand
concentration and fitting to a sigmoidal (Hill-type) binding model.
Compared to conventional techniques such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and biolayer interferometry (BLI)12, FLOWER offers several
distinct advantages for GPCR drug discovery:
• Label-free detection, avoiding the need for bulky or perturbative tags38;
• Ultra-high sensitivity, capable of detecting interactions at zeptomolar
concentrations15 or even single molecules16;
• Compatibility with complex biological samples, including detergentsolubilized receptors and crude membrane preparations15;
• Preservation of receptor conformation, supporting analysis of GPCRs
embedded in reconstituted lipid bilayers, nanodiscs, or other nativelike environments39.
These features are particularly advantageous in early-stage drug discovery, where receptor materials are often limited, interactions can be lowaffinity or transient. Here, maintaining membrane protein integrity is
crucial for biologically meaningful measurements. In the FLOWER assay,
the receptors are maintained in a membrane-like lipid environment
throughout the measurement. Purified GPCRs are first solubilized in mild
detergents that preserve transmembrane structure and then reconstituted
into supported lipid bilayers on the microtoroid surface through gradual
detergent removal. This appears to mimic the physiological cell membrane,
ensuring that the hydrophobic transmembrane regions of the receptor are
exposed to a lipid environment rather than the solid interface. In previous
studies, we have also successfully incorporated GPCRs into nanodiscs39,
which further confirmed that receptor folding and ligand-binding activity
are preserved in both membrane-mimetic formats. As shown in previous
work with other GPCRs (e.g., κ-opioid receptor15), receptors reconstituted
by this approach maintain ligand-specific binding and kinetic behavior,
confirmingfunctional integrity. The FLOWER platform measures label-free
binding via resonance shifts, and its ultra-high sensitivity (zeptomolar-level)
allows reliable measurement of receptor–ligand interactions even at very
low surface densities. Because detection depends on refractive-index perturbation near the resonator rather than on optical labels or ensemble
averaging, the assay is compatible with monomeric or multimeric receptor
complexes, including GPCR dimers or receptor–G-protein assemblies,
provided they are incorporated within the SLB.
Application to agonist discovery at bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs)
For studies with TAS2R5, we studied multiple potential agonists and noted a
significant variance from the computationally derived predicted binding
energy and the potency of the agonists. As shown in Fig. 3a, the compounds
denoted T5-1, T5-8, and T5-14 have different EC50 values for stimulating
Ca2+ in human airway smooth muscle (HASM) cells. The signal transduction coefficients (log [Rmax/EC50]) differed by over 2-orders of magnitude between T5-8 and T5-14. When plotted as the computationally
derived binding energy vs signal transduction coefficient (Fig. 3b), most
compounds displayed a consistent relationship between the two (black
symbols). However, a few (blue symbols, particularly T5-14) had greater
computationally derived binding energies than would be predicted based on
signaling in cells. In FLOWER studies, the results followed that of the
computationally derived energies. This indicated that the compound, such
as T5-14 indeed bound to the receptor with high affinity, but some portion
of that binding did not contribute to activation. This might be because of the
energy of binding at the orthosteric sites plus the energy from “null” binding
(or even a pose consistent with surmountable antagonism). This discrepancy is appreciated by plotting the effects of the agonist on the outputs
from FLOWER and Ca2+ from live cells (Fig. 3c). Binding measured by
FLOWER with different cell signaling results can occur with antagonists,
inverse agonists, partial agonists, and allosteric modulators. We had

To assess this possibility, we utilized metadynamics (MetaD) simulations.
We identified a key transition underlying TAS2R5 activation which involves
ligand-induced opening of the Gα subunit, at the interface between the αhelical (AH) and Ras-like domains. This conformational change drives the
transition from a pre-coupled TAS2R5–G protein complex to the fully
active state leading to functional signaling. Crucially, the magnitude of the
energetic barrier associated with this domain opening varies dramatically
between the agonists, correlating with their functional potency. For the most
efficacious agonist, T5-8, Gα opening occurred with a stronglyfavorablefree
energy change (ΔG= −11.1 kcal/mol) and a low activation barrier, ΔG* =
3.4 kcal/mol. For the moderately active T5-1, the AH–Ras cleft expanded
from 22 Å to 31 Å with a more modest energy gain of −4.8 kcal/mol and a
higher barrier, ΔG* = 4.8 kcal/mol. For the least potent agonist, T5-14, the
cleft still opened, but the associated energy gain was minimal
(ΔG= −1.4 kcal/mol and ΔG* = 6.2 kcal/mol), showing very inefficient
activation despite stable binding in the pre-coupled complex (Fig. 4).
Not only do these findings reveal the well-recognized axiom that
agonist potency is not governed solely by binding affinity, but indicates its
dependence on the energetics of G protein activation, particularly the ability
to drive domain separation within Gα to promote GDP exposure to solvent
and subsequent signaling. Importantly, the pursuit of this mechanism was
made possible by obtaining a direct measurement of agonist-receptor
binding energy by FLOWER which prompted the additional computational
studies. This has led to a much more predictive method of deriving agonist
action that will promote discovery of novel TAS2R5 agonists. MetaD could
be used to further analyze the top hits from docking prior to cell based
assays, potentially decreasing the number of compounds to be studied.

Integration into the Expanded Workflow
As part of the expanded GPCR drug discovery workflow, FLOWER acts as
a final assessment of the binding predicted by in silico prediction and the
assessment of cellular functional assays. FLOWER’s ability to monitor
binding in real time provides access to association and dissociation rates, as
well as equilibrium binding constants, enabling detailed characterization of
ligand binding kinetics. Any atypical features of the fit data indicate the
need for further exploration of the mechanism of action even when cellbased assays appear consistent with the desired pharmacologic properties.
This level of resolution supports structure–activity relationship (SAR)
analysis by revealing how specific chemical modifications influence both
binding affinity and interaction dynamics. Such kinetic insights are critical
for rational lead optimization, particularly in guiding R-group modifications to enhance potency, residence time, and target selectivity. Optimization is envisioned as an iterative design–test cycle guided by both
computational modeling and experimental data, in which structural analogs are generated by modifying R-groups on the ligand scaffold to systematically explore chemical features that influence receptor binding.
Computational prediction and cell-based assays prioritize these R-group
variants, which can be refined by cellular assays with the final best cases
measured with FLOWER, all together providing quantitative binding
affinities and kinetic information that identify modifications that improve
target engagement. Compounds showing improved activation in cellbasedfunctional assays would then be evaluated by FLOWER to determine
whether the R-group changes enhance activity and receptor selectivity,
thereby guiding further refinement. Based on our published work to date,
an initial round of cell-based assays on the top computationally ranked
ligands has been sufficient to identify candidates with good activity which
can then be followed by FLOWER measurements to confirm measurable
binding and suitable affinity. FLOWER can be incorporated at several steps
in the workflow.We show it after the cell-based high-throughput screening
because the latter rapidly eliminates candidates from large libraries and is
currently less labor intensive. In addition to screening existing ligand
libraries, this workflow can also incorporate generative AI models to
propose de novo ligand structures based on the predicted receptor binding
pocket(s). Together, these capabilities position FLOWER to support both
early validation and future iterative optimization as more ligands are
explored.
While FLOWER offers a uniquely sensitive, label-free means of
monitoring receptor–ligand interactions in real time, several limitations
should be acknowledged. FLOWER quantifies physical binding events
rather than downstream signaling and therefore does not directly report
functional outcomes such as G-protein activation, second-messenger
production, or β-arrestin recruitment. Nevertheless, functional correlations can be inferred through experimental design. For example,
incorporation of mini-G proteins allows assessment of couplingdependent conformational changes that alter ligand-binding kinetics
and affinities, while competition assays on the same toroid enable discrimination between agonists and antagonists based on their relative
binding behaviors. The method also currently requires purified receptor
proteins reconstituted into stabilizing lipid environments such as
nanodiscs or supported bilayers, which may limit its applicability to
receptors that are difficult to express or purify. While the lipid environment of a cell cannot be perfectly matched with the FLOWER method,
we note that the lipid content of the cell membrane can differ by cell type
anyway so the absolute values may be shifted compared to the affinity
measured using a specific cell expressing the receptor (if it could be
measured). Furthermore, if known low affinity and high affinity ligands
are utilized as controls with each experiment, a rank-order of affinity (or
other outputs) can be established which places into perspective the
properties of tested ligands. Ultimately, signaling assays in cells and
physiologic studies in the relevant tissues or intact organisms remain
essential to establish whether ligand-induced receptor engagement
produces the desired therapeutic outcomes.
Future Directions
GPCR drug discovery is entering a transformative phase, enabled by
advances in structural biology, computational modeling, and highsensitivity experimental platforms. As our understanding of GPCR conformational dynamics and signaling bias expands, there is a growing need
for integrated workflows that iteratively combine prediction, measurement, and validation. Technologies like FLOWER will play a central role in
this evolution. Its ability to provide real-time, label-free, and ultra-sensitive
kinetic measurements makes it particularly well-suited for early studies
especially when receptor quantities are limiting or interactions are weak
and transient. On the other hand, cell-based studies are essential to distinguish cases that may have high activation barriers or multimodal
binding. Future development of FLOWER may include miniaturization,
parallelization, and integration with microfluidic platforms, enabling highthroughput assays at unprecedented resolution. Coupling FLOWER with
AI-driven compound design and automated feedback loops can further
accelerate discovery. The integration of computational prediction, kinetic
validation via FLOWER, and downstream functional assays forms a
closed-loop pipeline that optimizes both ligand potency and specificity in
real time. Overall, FLOWER exemplifies a new class of biophysical tools
that bridge traditional divides between computation and experiment, as
well as early discovery and preclinical validation. As workflows become
more integrated, scalable, and physiologically relevant, we anticipate a
substantial acceleration in the development of GPCR-targeted therapeutics
across diverse disease areas.
