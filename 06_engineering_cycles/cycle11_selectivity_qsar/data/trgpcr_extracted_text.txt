TrGPCR: GPCR–Ligand Binding Affinity
Prediction Based on Dynamic Deep
Transfer Learning
Yaoyao Lu , Runhua Zhang , Tengsheng Jiang , Qiming Fu , Zhiming Cui, and Hongjie Wu
Abstract—Predicting G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR)
–ligand binding affinity plays a crucial role in drug development. However, determining GPCR–ligand binding affinities
is time-consuming and resource-intensive. Although many
studies used data-driven methods to predict binding affinity, most of these methods required protein 3D structure,
which was often unknown. Moreover, part of these studies
only considered the sequence characteristics of the protein, ignoring the secondary structure of the protein. The
number of known GPCR for affinity prediction is only a
few thousand, which is insufficient for deep learning training. Therefore, this study aimed to propose a deep transfer learning method called TrGPCR, which used dynamic
transfer learning to solve the problem of insufficient GPCR
data. We used the Binding Database (BindingDB) as the
source domain and the GLASS (GPCR-Ligand Association)
database as the target domain. We also introduced protein
secondary structures, called pockets, as features to predict
binding affinities. Compared with DeepDTA, our model improved by 5.2% on RMSE (root mean square error) and 4.5%
on MAE (mean squared error).
Index Terms—Binding affinity, deep learning, GPCR,
transfer learning.

I. INTRODUCTION
DRUG retargeting (DR) has gained attraction due to its
shorter time, lower investment, and higher success rate
than traditional drug development from scratch. This realization and the vast repository of drug-related data accessible to
researchers have led to the development of many computational
DR strategies [1], [2]. G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) is a
membrane protein essential in cell signaling. GPCRs play a key
role in intracellular communication and are highly controllable
drug targets [3]. Of the 3048 GPCR entries in GLASS extracted
from Uniport [4], only 681 GPCRs have ligand association [5].
Therefore, the existing GPCR data pair has the problem of
lack of experimental quantity [6]. Characterizing compound–
protein interactions (CPI) is critical for drug discovery and
development [7]. A library of compounds can be screened for a
given protein target or tested for a given compound [8] against
possible non-target proteins. Besides predicting the binary interaction between compound–protein pairs, predicting the binding affinity of compound–protein pairs, which is also known
as drug–targeted affinity (DTA), remains a challenge in drug
discovery [9].
The intensity of the interaction between drug–target (DT)
pairs is usually expressed as measures such as dissociation constant (Kd)[10], inhibition constant (Ki)[11], or semi-maximum
inhibitory concentration (IC50) [12]. IC50 depends on the concentration of the target and ligand [13], and a low IC50 value
indicates strong binding. Similarly, lower Ki values indicate
higher binding affinity. Kd and Ki values are usually expressed
in pKd and pKi respectively, which is the negative logarithm of
dissociation or suppression constants.
Studying the protein–ligand interactions is critical for drug
development [14]. Identifying binding ligands from large-scale
chemical spaces using current experimental methods [15], such
as nonparametric machine learning methods [16], [17], [18],
[19], [20] and Random Forest (RF) algorithms [21] using features such as atomic pair coexistence, and so forth, is still
challenging. This has oversimplified the description of protein–
ligand complexes and resulted in a loss of information that the
original interacting complexes might provide. Support vector
machine (SVM) algorithms [22] for predicting protein–ligand
interactions mainly focus on binary classification [23]. In recent
years, deep learning architectures have also become a popular
choice in drug discovery research. Deep learning-based research

and CNN(Convolutional Neural Network)-based architectures,
using detailed information provided by 3D structures [25],
[26], [27] rather than the traditional machine learning methods. The structure-based approaches, such as Pafnucy [28],
DeepAtom[29], and TopologyNet[30]require detailed information on each molecule atom while being limited to the available

the three-dimensional structure of ligand–protein complexes is
known to comprise only about 30000 compounds [32], and
some structureless methods have been proposed to overcome
structure-based limitations. DeepDTA [12] and WideDTA [33]
are models that rely on protein sequences and ligand Simplified

disregarding other crucial information. DeepDTAF [34] takes
into account the secondary structural characteristics and physicochemical properties of proteins, improving the expression of
protein features. However, insufficient samples in processing
GPCRs is still an issue, and high-performance models cannot
be trained.
Transfer learning is a machine learning method that aims
to improve learning performance on another related domain,
known as the target domain, by learning from what is learned in
one domain (called the source domain). The core idea of transfer
learning is to use the experience of the source domain to help the
learning tasks of the target domain by applying the knowledge
or models learned on the source domain to the target domain.
In this article, the GPCR-ligand we need to deal with has the
problem of insufficient samples, while the amount of data in
protein-ligand is sufficient, which is a good application area for
transfer learning.
In this study, we proposed a new deep learning-based model
consisting of three separate modules: the whole protein module,
the pocket module, and the ligand SMILES module [35]. Just
like DeepDTA, which does not use 3D structures, we employed
protein sequences and ligand SMILES representations. Additionally, we took the the secondary structure of the protein into
account as a feature to nhance the characterization of the protein.
Considering that GPCR–ligand pairs are smaller in number,
and deep learning tends to require a larger sample size, we
employed the transfer learning Dynamic TrAdaBoost algorithm.
This allowed us to extract data with a sample distribution similar
to that of the BindingDB, thereby expanding the sample size for
predictive combined affinity. We used CNNs and multiscale convolutional neural network (MCNN) [36] blocks for modeling.
Subsequently, we evaluated the performance of the model on
the provided data and conducted tests to verify its generalization lcapability. These results suggested that our method could
provide reliable predictions of GPCR–ligand binding affinity.
II. METHOD
A. Framework of DTA Prediction Based on Dynamic
Deep Transfer Learning
Our experiments combined transfer learning methods with
deep learning methods. First, considering that the amount of
GPCR–ligand data verified by the experiment was slightly insufficient for deep learning, we used a transfer learning algorithm
to extract the dataset related to the target sample to augment the
sample. The new enriched dataset was trained through a deep
learning network to meet our regression task combined with
affinity prediction.
In the transfer learning module, we select BindingDB as the
source domain and GLASS as the target domain. All protein and
ligand pairs are then encoded. We then train the classifiers using
the sequence features of the protein and the SMILES features of
the ligand. Finally, samples with high similarity are separated by
sample weights and classifier weight pairs, and combined with
the initial target domain to form a new dataset.
In the deep learning module, we added a Pocket module for
the Pocket representation of the dataset and extracted features
using CNNs. We used MCNN to extract features for the Protein
Sequence Module and Ligand Module.First, pocket information,
protein sequence and ligand SMILEs will be input into the
network, pockets will extract features by CNN, the rest will
be extracted by MCNN, MCNN can extract local and global
features, we will extract extracted structure expression, protein
expression and ligand expression fusion input into the fully
connected layer for information integration, and finally output
the prediction results. The specific framework is shown in Fig. 1.
B. Problem Description
Predicting the DTI(Drug-Target Interactions) is the task of
recommending a system or link prediction for a two-part network [37]. DTIs datasets consist of drugs D={d1,d2,...,dn}
and targets T = {t1,t2,...,tm}. The n × m adjacency matrix Y
∈ Rn×m represents the drug–target interaction. Y i,j = 1 means
that the drug di(1 ≤ i ≤ n) interacts with the target tj (1 ≤
j ≤ m), the opposite is 0. Let F* ∈ Rn×m be the fractional
function matrix. F∗
i,j are the scores of drug di and target tj
interactions (links to interaction networks). The problem is to
estimate F*.The prediction DTA problem is not a simple 0,1
problem on this basis, but a regression problem that predicts
specific values. In our study, a pair of drug–target interactions
was represented as an example. So, each element in the adjacent
matrix Y ∈ Rn×m was a sample.
C. Feature Construction
In this study, tag encoding was employed using only 1D
sequence data. The input representation did not include the 3D
structure of proteins, ligands, or their complexes. To enhance the
efficiency of capturing interactive information, we used three
partial representations: pocket representation, protein sequence
representation, and ligand representation. In a previous study,
the protein sequences and ligand SMILES were proven to be
effective methods for predicting protein–ligand binding affinity [12]. On this basis, we considered the structural property
pocket information. The details of the input information are
provided in the following text.
1) Pocket Representation: Pockets usually refer to binding
cavities inside or on the surface of proteins, with some special
physicochemical and geometric properties that bind to small

Transfer learning aims to improve the learning of new target
concepts [38] where relevant source concepts are known. The
idea behind transfer learning is that it is inspired by human
learning, and the principle behind it is described in a previous
study [39]. If you are already familiar with a concept (e.g.,
trumpet blowing), it will be easier when you learn a similar new

concept (e.g., how to play a trombone). The transfer learning
method refers to a learning process that uses the similarities
between data, tasks, or models to apply models learned in the
old field to the new field. Considering that most of the data or
tasks are related, we can share the learned model parameters
with the new model through transfer learning in some way to
accelerate and optimize the learning efficiency of the model
without learning from scratch like most models, or use the
similarity between the data to expand some small-sample data
to achieve the effect of increasing the sample size, thereby
improving the generalization and prediction ability of the model.
The TrAdaBoost algorithm [39] can solve the problem of
different sample distributions by adjusting the sample weight for
screening. Although TrAdaBoost can get the classifier we need,
it has some flaws and deficiencies. For example, TrAdaBoost
produces polarization problems, which are situations where the
weight of the source domain sample is significantly increased
and the weight of the target domain sample decreases to close
to 0. This weight adjustment can cause the model to focus
too much on the source domain and fail to make full use of
the information in the target domain, resulting in degraded
learning performance on the target domain. In our experiments,
the distribution of the source and target domains was uniform,
polarization affected the transmission process, and the problem
of the source domain weights changing during this process made
the TrAdaBoost algorithm classifier prone to overconcentration
in difficult samples. These factors degraded the performance of
the classifier of the TrAdaBoost algorithm. This contradicted
machine learning [18].
To solve this problem, we proposed a dynamic learning
method. We put the updated data into the Dynamic TrAdaBoost
algorithm. Based on the error of the target domain, we added
a dynamic factor to the weight to update the source domain
weight so as to solve the polarization problem between the
target domain and the source domain. For example, in the 50th
epoch of update, the protein (PDB: 3F5P) was 2.91341614e-05
without the the correction factor, which was much larger than
the average weight, and after introduction, it was reduced to
2.61423183e-05, which greatly reduced the weight ratio.The
specific algorithm is shown in Algorithm. 1.
Considering that the TrAdaBoost algorithm causes a continuous decrease in the source domain data weight, resulting in a polarization problem, we used the 9th update strategy, multiplying
the weight update by a Ct
, which we called the correction factor.
This proved that the strategy could effectively address the issue
of the source domain sample convergence weights progressing
too quickly. It was observed that the update of target sample
weights was not related to the correction factor. According
to the transfer learning paradigm, the source distribution was
correlated and the target instance could benefit from merging
related source instances.

F. Training Process
1) Transfer Process: In the Dynamic TrAdaBoost experiment, we used the default decision tree classifier. We set the
hyperparameter of the decision tree max_depth to 10. Regarding
source domains, we used 61,258 pieces of protein–ligand interaction data from the BindingDB as the source domain data and
8742 pieces of interaction data from the GLASS GPCR–ligand
for the target domain. We set up 50 weak classifiers. The learning
rate was 0.03, and the number of iterations was 50 times.
After 50 iterations of the weak classifier, we obtained the
weights of different samples and the weights of the classifier. The
larger the weight of the sample, the more similar the distribution
space was to the target domain, which was the data we needed.
Based on these weights, we extracted sample pairs that exceeded
the mean and obtained 17,955 pieces of data that would be
trained in a deep network along with the target domain data.
The time complexity of Dynamic TrAdaBoost is mainly divided into three parts, data preprocessing, base classifier training, and Dynamic TrAdaBoost iteration. The data preprocessing
complexity is O(n * m), where n is the number of samples and
m is the number of features. The time complexity of training
a single base classifier is O(f(n, m)), where f is a function
that depends on the size of the dataset and the number of
features. Assuming k iterations, the overall time complexity of
the Dynamic TrAdaBoost algorithm can be approximated as O(k
* (n * f(n, m) + n)).
2) Neural Network Architecture: We used 5-fold crossvalidation to determine model hyperparameters. We used three
1D traditional convolutions and increased the number of filters.
We design 3 layers of CNN, the number of convolution kernels
in each layer is 32, 64, 128, and the number of convolution
kernels of the three layers of MCNN is the same, and the size
of the convolution kernel is unified to 3, that is, one layer of
convolution will integrate three units of information. The whole
experiment was debugged for 100 rounds, and the learning rate
was 0.001.The specific parameters are shown in Table I.

III. DATASETS
A. Binding Database
BindingDB: https://www.bindingdb.org/ is a publicly available, web-accessible database of measurements of binding affinity that focuses on the interaction of proteins considered drug
targets with small molecules resembling drugs. BindingDB
data comes from PDB(Protein Data Bank) -related literature
report data, patent information, PubChem BioAssays data, and
ChEMBL record data. As of June 26, 2022, BindingDB contained 41,296 entries, each with a DOI, containing 8811 protein targets and 2,533,459 binding data for 1,085,985 small
molecules. A total of 5988 protein–ligand crystal structures perform BindingDB affinity measurements on proteins with 100%
sequence identity, whereas 11,442 crystal structures provide proteins with 85% sequence identity. The affinity data come from a
variety of measurement techniques, including enzyme inhibitory
activity and enzyme kinetics, isothermal titration calorimetry,
nuclear magnetic resonance, and radioligand competition assays, including Ki, IC50, Kd, EC50, and so forth.
The BindingDB dataset contains 39747 positive examples
and 31218 negative examples from a public database [46].

B. GLASS
GLASS is a comprehensive database of experimentally validated GPCR-ligand associations. GPCRs are probably the most
attractive drug-target membrane proteins, accounting for a large
portion of drug targets in the contemporary drug discovery
industry [5]. GPCRs are one of the largest families of transmembrane proteins associated with many human diseases, such
as cancer and diabetes [47]. One of the difficulties in developing
GPCR-ligand interaction pairs to advance biological research
is that the ligand associations for GPCRs are diverse and often
mixed with various false positives [5].
Based on the aforementioned questions, Wallace K. B. Chan
et al. created a website that provided GPCR–ligand associations
by mining GPCR-ligand interactions in the PubMed literature
and then cross-examining them with the main pharmacological dataset (https://zhanggroup.org/GLASS/). The affinities on
GLASS are expressed by Ki, Kd, IC50 and EC50. Ki is the
inhibition constant, that is, the affinity of the ligand to the
receptor, the lower the value of Ki, the higher its affinity. Kd
is the dissociation constant that describes how tightly the ligand
binds to the receptor. Similarly, lower values correspond to
tighter binding of ligands to receptors. IC50 is half the maximum
inhibitory concentration and measures the drug concentration
required for maximum functional inhibition of 50%. Lower
values correspond to more effective inhibitors. EC50 is half
the maximum effective concentration and measures 50% of the
concentration of drugs required for the maximum functional
response. Lower values correspond to more effective agonists.
We required positive and negative samples of GPCR–ligand
pairs and protein–ligand pairs for our study. To account for noise
and undetected bias, we randomly cross-combined CPI pairs
or used similarity-based methods. These approaches were used
to construct the necessary data for the TrAdaBoost algorithm.
Therefore, the Label Inversion Dataset was constructed following two rules: (1) data should be collected from experimentally
validated databases, and (2) each ligand should be present in
both positive and negative samples. We used the Kd value and
converted it into the logarithmic space pKd value as binding

substantial impact kernel size can have on our model. So we
experimentally compared convolution kernels of different sizes

lower than the CI (0.505) of kernel = 5, it was clear that the
kernel = 3 was the best parameter.

V. DISCUSSION
In this study, we proposed a novel model for predicting the
GPCR–ligand binding affinity. Our model combined CNN and
MCNN to extract multiscale features from both the protein and
the ligand. Previous studies primarily focused on drug–target
interactions, with limited studies specifically targeting GPCRs
due to the scarcity of GPCR data. However, given the importance
of GPCRs as therapeutic targets in the human body, studying
GPCRs holds great significance for understanding essential
biological processes. We integrated deep learning models with
transfer learning to address the challenge of limited GPCR
samples, providing a methodology to overcome the data scarcity
issue. Our model achieved higher prediction accuracy compared
with other models.
The number of available three-dimensional protein structures
is significantly lower than the number of protein sequences. This
limitation restricts the application of structure-based prediction
methods. Therefore, our proposed method focused on extracting
features from the primary and secondary structures of proteins,
without relying on the three-dimensional protein structures.
Our approach had broader applicability compared with methods
relying on three-dimensional structures.
The contribution of this article can be summarized in the
following three points. First, the number of GPCR-ligands
verified by experiments is only a few hundred, and the deep
learning network cannot be trained, we introduce a sample
transfer algorithm, migrate the relevant data set from Binding
DB, initialize the JS divergence with the sample weight, and
introduce dynamic factors to alleviate the polarization problem.
Second, we propose a new combinatorial model that uses CNN
and MCNN for feature extraction, respectively, and finally fuses
feature expression for prediction tasks. Third, without using
the three-dimensional structure of proteins, we consider the
influence of protein secondary structure properties on binding
affinity, input the secondary structure as additional features to
the model, and obtain better performance.
Although our method successfully addressed the issue of
limited training samples and improved prediction performance,
it still has room for improvement. First, although we used the
BindingDB as the source domain for transfer learning, exploring
multiple diverse and relevant databases as the source domain is a
future direction. Second, in terms of protein secondary structure
selection, we only considered four typical attributes. Extracting
features from additional attributes and incorporating physicochemical properties and three-dimensional structural information of proteins are potential enhancements. Therefore, in future
studies, we aim to model protein characteristics by considering
the physicochemical properties and three-dimensional structural
features of proteins.
VI. CONCLUSION
Considering that a small amount of experimentally verified
GPCR data may not be able to achieve the best performance of
the deep learning model, we used sample-based dynamic transfer
to extend the dataset, which was optimized for data initialization
weight, iterative process data weight adjustment, and classifier
selection. In terms of deep learning, we proposed a combined
model based on CNN and MCNN to predict GPCR–ligand binding affinity. In affinity prediction tasks, current depth algorithms
mostly use only protein and ligand sequences, without considering other properties of proteins. Therefore, we added structural
information called Pocket module. The experimental results
showed that the performance of the prediction method using
dynamic transfer learning was significantly higher than that of
the method without transfer learning. Regarding feature selection, the performance while using sequence-level and secondary
structures can also be better than that of partial three-dimensional
structure methods, and it is more convenient to obtain features.
We also tested the generalization of our model on other datasets.
The experimental results showed that the proposed TrGPCR was
a reliable GPCR–ligand binding affinity prediction method.
There are also some exciting research directions in the approach we use. The first is the improvement and optimization
of the model, which improves the architecture and design of the
MCNN model by introducing attention mechanisms, increasing the depth or width of the network, using new activation
functions, etc., to improve accuracy, efficiency, and robustness.
